# Reference Selection and Panel Rubric

Use this rubric to evaluate candidate functional and visual references before the implementation direction is locked. The selection panel has a maximum of three passes total: initial review plus up to two refinement rounds.

## Functional references

Assess each candidate for:

- **Product and workflow relevance:** Does it solve a problem, support a user journey, or present information in a way that transfers to this brief?
- **Observable evidence:** Were the relevant pages or interactions actually inspected? What could not be accessed?
- **Interaction quality:** Are controls, transitions, navigation, states, and feedback clear and purposeful?
- **Information architecture:** Is the content hierarchy, navigation model, or information density useful for the target audience?
- **Responsive quality:** Is there evidence of thoughtful behavior at more than one viewport?
- **Distinct contribution:** Does it offer a lesson not already represented by another candidate?
- **Transferability:** Can the lesson be adapted without copying the reference's unique branding or structure wholesale?

A candidate that is merely popular, visually fashionable, or top-ranked in search is not automatically a strong reference. Prefer concrete patterns that answer a problem posed by the brief.

## Visual references

Assess each candidate for:

- **Art direction:** Does it have a coherent, recognisable visual point of view?
- **Hierarchy and composition:** Does it organise attention and content effectively?
- **Typography and colour:** Are these choices distinctive, readable, and appropriate to the product?
- **Spacing and density:** Does the composition feel intentional rather than default or arbitrary?
- **Real-product transferability:** Could the design language survive real content, responsive layouts, and interactive states?
- **Distinct contribution:** Does it bring a useful visual idea not already covered by other references?
- **Originality risk:** Would borrowing this pattern too closely make the result derivative or dependent on a recognizable brand identity?

Do not reject a restrained design because it lacks decoration. Do not accept a concept solely because it is polished in a static image.

## Reference panel procedure

Before the review, provide every reviewer with the original task brief, the relevant reference URLs and screenshots when available, a short note about what the lead agent intends to learn from each reference, and any meaningful constraints from the repository.

Ask reviewers to return:

1. **Verdict:** Accept, refine, or replace the set.
2. **Strongest evidence:** Specific references and patterns that solve actual needs.
3. **Weaknesses:** Irrelevant, redundant, inaccessible, impractical, generic, or derivative references, with evidence.
4. **Gaps:** Important product/design questions not represented by the set.
5. **Recommended changes:** Concrete substitutions or changes to the synthesis plan.
6. **Confidence and limitations:** What they actually inspected versus inferred.

The lead agent must adjudicate the findings, not simply count votes. Give more weight to reproducible evidence and fit with the brief than to unsupported preference. Reviewers may disagree, but their disagreements should identify the actual trade-off.

## Pass limits and stopping rule

- Pass 1: independently assess the proposed references and coverage.
- Pass 2: evaluate the revised set if Pass 1 found material weaknesses.
- Pass 3: final bounded check of remaining high-impact concerns.

Do not perform a fourth reference-panel pass. After the third pass, resolve remaining uncertainty using the strongest available evidence and proceed. This limit does **not** apply to the final implementation review, which continues until acceptance or a genuine blocker.

## Selection quality bar

The accepted set should collectively provide meaningful coverage of product behavior and visual direction. Prefer references with complementary strengths. A useful final mapping might identify one source for navigation, another for a dense data view, another for form behavior, and visual sources for typography, composition, or art direction. The synthesis must explain how these lessons support one coherent identity.
